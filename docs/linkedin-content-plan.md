# LinkedIn Content Plan

Created: 2026-08-01

Fifteen posts: one demo video, fourteen supporting. Briefs only — no copy written yet. Each brief carries the elements a script needs, so turning one into a draft is a writing job, not a thinking job.

## What is being sold

**Custom AI automation, built and implemented inside a client's existing business.** Not software. Not a SaaS product. Nothing to log into, no seats, no subscription.

This governs every post. The COI system is a **worked example of how I approach a problem** — evidence of judgment, not a product being launched. Language never implies "my tool" or "my platform." It says: this is the kind of thing I build, inside your mailbox, your database, your process, and you own it when it's done.

**The buyer:** US residential property management companies, roughly 150–600 doors. Owners and operations leads.

**The competitive context.** COI tracking is an established software category — myCOI, NetVendor, VendorAccess, BCS, SmartCompliance, ExpiryEdge, Jones, Vertikal RMS, Lula. Property managers researching this problem are already being marketed to heavily, so no post should imply nobody has noticed the problem. Every one of those products is a portal, and the category's own documented failure is that vendors find portals cumbersome and stop cooperating. That is the opening: same problem, no system for anyone to adopt. See `customer-language.md` for the vocabulary, the worn-out category words to avoid, and the terminology rules.

**Where each post leads:** authority first, conversation second, call third. Only two posts carry a real CTA. The rest earn the right to one.

## The accessibility arc

Posts 1–5 are written so that someone with no property management knowledge — a friend, a former colleague, a general connection — understands within one line what world this is about and who it's for. No jargon before it's earned. A property manager reading them should feel described; everyone else should feel let in.

This matters because early on, the people seeing these posts are the wrong audience by default. The gateway posts still work on them: they explain the domain rather than assuming it, and they make the target reader unmistakable. That means these five can keep running while commenting and connection work slowly brings actual property managers into the room.

Posts 6–15 assume the reader knows what a certificate of insurance is. Publish those once profile visits and comments start coming from the industry.

**No origin story.** The work explains itself. Positioning comes from what the posts demonstrate, not from a personal narrative that would need rewriting for every future build.

Carousels carry the explaining — they are the best format for making an unfamiliar domain legible, which is exactly what the gateway posts need. Video carries trust, which is what a stranger overseas needs most. Text is the backbone and the cheapest to produce, which matters when the same person is also building.

## Pillars

| Pillar | Count | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Build in public | 4 | Proof the work is real |
| Operating model | 3 | How an engagement actually works — the pillar a services business needs and a product business doesn't |
| Honest limitation | 3 | What I refuse to build. The clearest signal I'm not a spam vendor |
| Industry literacy | 3 | Domain knowledge a generic automation freelancer would never have |
| Teardown | 2 | Where the time and money actually go |

**Operating model is new** and exists because of the services reframe. Posts about how the work happens — what I need before building, what an automation is allowed to do alone, why it runs on your infrastructure — are impossible for a product seller to write and are the posts that most directly produce calls.

No standalone question post. Engagement questions are folded into the closing line of posts 1 and 4 instead of spending a slot.

## Process and frameworks

Run in this order. Steps 0 and 1 happen once; the rest repeat per batch.

| Step | Source | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | `product-marketing` | Creates `.agents/product-marketing.md`. Over 100 skill files read this before doing anything, so writing it once stops every later step from guessing at audience, positioning, and voice |
| 1 | `customer-research` | Real property manager phrasing harvested from Property Management Mastermind and r/propertymanagement. Fills the Customer Language section. **Without this every draft uses my vocabulary for this industry, not theirs** |
| 2 | `social` references | Structure per format — carousel frameworks, video scripting template, the 210-character fold |
| 3 | `copywriting` | Line-level principles and style rules, plus `references/natural-transitions.md` for transitions that don't signpost |
| 4 | `offers` | Post 15 only. Value framing for the free diagnostic, and its guidance on when *not* to apply offer tactics |
| 5 | `seo-audit/references/ai-writing-detection.md` | **Mandatory pass on every draft.** Em dashes, overused verbs and adjectives, banned phrases, structural patterns |
| 6 | `image`, plus `high-end-visual-design` | Carousel slides, the two image posts, profile banner. The design skills exist to avoid the generic template look, which is its own tell |
| 7 | `copy-editing` | Seven Sweeps, after you have rewritten the draft in your own words |

`full-output-enforcement` applies to every batch: no partial delivery, no "the rest follows the same pattern."

**Deliberately not used:** `marketing-psychology` (persuasion levers fight an understated positioning), `video` (production tooling, not scripting), `ads` and `ad-creative` (paid), `content-strategy` (this plan is the output), `lead-magnets` (subsumed by `offers`), `marketing-council` (a simulated advisory board produces confident generic advice).

**Rejected from skills otherwise in use:** `social`'s hook libraries, for both text and video. They generate "Unpopular opinion:", "I was wrong about X," "[Number] things that [outcome]" — the templated register that reads as machine-written. Structure yes, hooks no. Hooks come from the material. The same applies to `post-templates.md`, whose skeletons are sound but which end every template in engagement bait ("Which resonates most with you?"). Use the skeleton, cut the closer.

## Brief format

Every post below has the same six fields, matching the structure the scripts will use:

- **Hook angle** — what the opening does, not the words
- **The turn** — the pivot the whole post is built on
- **Evidence** — the specifics that must appear, with source
- **Takeaway** — what the reader leaves holding
- **Legibility** — for gateway posts, how an outsider follows it
- **CTA**

# Gateway posts (1–5)

Accessible to anyone. Unmistakably aimed at property managers.

## 1. What a property manager actually does all day

**Format:** carousel, 8 slides · **Framework:** Value-Stack · **Pillar:** teardown · **CTA:** soft — invite the industry to correct or add

**Why Value-Stack:** the cover states an exact count and completeness is the promise. LinkedIn shows a "N pages" badge on document posts, which makes exact-count covers land harder.

**Hook angle:** Open on the gap between what people assume the job is (showing apartments, collecting rent) and what it actually is. No automation mentioned anywhere in this post.

**The turn:** Almost none of the job is property. It's paperwork with deadlines attached — vendor documents, maintenance requests, renewals, owner questions, move-out timelines — and every one of them has a date that matters and a person who has to chase it.

**Evidence:** The operational areas from the problem inventory: vendor documents, maintenance triage, leasing paperwork, renewals, delinquency follow-up, owner reporting, move in/out, notice timing. One slide per cluster, phrased as the task not the category.

**Takeaway:** For a general reader — oh, that's what the job is. For a property manager — someone finally described my week correctly.

**Legibility:** Zero domain knowledge required. Explains the world from scratch. This is the post that makes every later post make sense.

**Why first:** It establishes the audience without an origin story. Nobody has to wonder who these posts are for after reading it.

## 2. The demo — a certificate arrives and the register updates itself

**Format:** native video, 2–3 minutes · **Framework:** scene structure from `demo-script.md` · **Pillar:** build in public · **CTA:** soft — offer to walk through it

**Why not the short-form template:** at 2–3 minutes this sits outside short-form video, where the 0–3 second hook rules apply. The demo script already has a scene structure built for this length.

**Hook angle:** Open on the finished board, then immediately do the thing. No preamble, no "hi, I'm…"

**The turn:** Nothing here required anyone to type. A document arrived, the facts came out of it, the register changed, and when a reminder needed sending it stopped and waited for a person.

**Evidence:** Structure per the demo cut — the problem in one line, one certificate uploaded, the four states (current, expiring, expired, needs review), the source PDF one click away, the approval email with two buttons, and twenty seconds at the end on what it does not do.

**Takeaway:** This is real, it works, and the person who built it volunteers its limits.

**Legibility:** Watching software work needs no domain knowledge. The closing limitation line is what makes it credible rather than promotional.

**Framing discipline:** "Here's something I built for a property management company's vendor paperwork." Never "here's my product." The video is evidence of how I work, not a launch.

## 3. Five things I would refuse to automate in a property management business

**Format:** text, 250–300 words · **Skeleton:** List Post, closer cut · **Pillar:** honest limitation · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** State plainly that there are things in this business I would turn down work on, and that I'd turn them down even though the money spends the same.

**The turn:** The limit isn't technical. Every one of these is buildable. The reason not to build them is that the cost of being wrong lands on someone who didn't choose the risk — a resident, an owner, a vendor.

**Evidence:** Anything that decides which vendor gets dispatched. Anything that sends a legal notice on a timer. Anything that judges a tenant. Anything that tells an owner money is fine. Anything that decides a vendor is insured — including the system I actually built, which deliberately refuses to.

**Takeaway:** The person you want building this is the one who tells you what not to build.

**Legibility:** Reads as judgment, not jargon. Works on any reader, and it's the post most likely to be shared by someone outside the industry.

## 4. The four-minute problem

**Format:** carousel, 7 slides · **Framework:** Problem-Proof · **Pillar:** teardown · **CTA:** soft — ask which four-minute task they'd hand over

**Why Problem-Proof:** slide 1 opens on a claim with a number, the middle slides carry the arithmetic as the mechanism, and the last slide closes the loop with the total. The open loop is what drives the swipe.

**Hook angle:** Open on a single unremarkable task that takes four minutes. Nobody would ever automate a four-minute task.

**The turn:** It doesn't happen once. It happens forty times a month, spread across three people, and it never appears on anyone's list because each instance is too small to count. Work that disappears is never the big stuff.

**Evidence:** The arithmetic, done honestly: chasing one vendor certificate is four minutes; a 300-door manager does it repeatedly across dozens of vendors; the second and third follow-up are the invisible part because they feel like nothing. Land on the monthly total without inflating it.

**Takeaway:** The tasks worth automating are the ones too small to notice, not the ones big enough to complain about.

**Legibility:** Universal. Anyone who has a job recognizes this immediately, and it frames why custom automation exists without pitching anything.

---

## 5. I don't sell software

**Format:** text, 200–250 words · **Skeleton:** Contrarian Take, closer cut · **Pillar:** operating model · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** State the negative first. There's nothing to log into, no seats, no monthly fee for a dashboard nobody opens.

**The turn:** What I build runs inside the business that already exists — the mailbox you already use, a database you own, the process your team already follows. When it's finished it belongs to you, and if we stop working together it keeps running.

**Evidence:** Concretely, what "inside your business" means: it reads the mailbox you already have, writes to a database you control, sends from your address, and can be exported and handed to anyone. No vendor lock, no data hostage.

**Takeaway:** This is a different transaction from buying a tool. You're not adopting a product; you're getting a specific piece of your operation handled.

**Legibility:** This is the post that makes the whole offer make sense to anyone who assumed I was selling an app. It should exist early precisely because that assumption is the default.

# Industry posts (6–15)

Assume the reader knows what a certificate of insurance is. Publish once industry visitors start arriving.

## 6. Nobody has to tell you when a policy is cancelled

**Format:** text, 200–250 words · **Structure:** false belief → the fact → what it changes · **Pillar:** industry literacy · **CTA:** soft — offer the longer write-up

**Hook angle:** Open on the false sense of security: a certificate in the file showing twelve months of coverage.

**The turn:** The 2010 ACORD revision removed the carrier's obligation to notify certificate holders of cancellation. That certificate can describe a policy that ended in March, and nobody is required to say a word.

**Evidence:** The 2010 revision. The current ACORD 25 filing language stating the certificate is informational and does not amend the policy. Texas DOI guidance that notice depends on policy or endorsement terms. Sources at the foot of `docs/demo-script.md`.

**Takeaway:** The certificate proves what was true the day it was issued. Nothing after.

**Why this one matters most:** It's the single most useful thing you can tell a property manager who has never been told it, and it's unfakeable domain knowledge.

## 7. Twelve things that can be wrong with a certificate

**Format:** carousel, 10 slides · **Framework:** Value-Stack · **Pillar:** honest limitation · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Cover states the exact count and the split. Completeness is the promise.

**The turn:** Seven of them should stop you and get a person involved. Five should be noted and filed. A process that treats all twelve the same trains everyone to ignore it.

**Evidence:** Stop-cold: expiration before effective date, an impossible date, illegible or ambiguous fields, not a certificate at all, older than what's on file, a vendor name one keystroke from an existing one, a password-protected file. File-with-note: holder not on your accepted list, a limit under your minimum, no general liability line, no workers' comp line, a renewal that leaves a gap. Source: `scripts/lib/coi-stage-4-validator.js`.

**Takeaway:** Sorting problems by severity is the actual work. Anything can flag everything.

**Production note:** Exclude the two internal model failure modes. A property manager doesn't care that a model misbehaved, and including them turns a hand-checkable list into a spec sheet.

## 8. The reminder that fired twice in two days

**Format:** text, 250–300 words · **Skeleton:** Story Post, closer cut · **Pillar:** build in public · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on the scene — a vendor got two near-identical reminders a day apart — and immediately say nothing was broken.

**The turn:** Every rule fired exactly as written. The flaw was in how I'd defined "the same problem." Buckets were part of the definition, so a certificate crossing from fifteen days out to fourteen became a brand new problem, and the seven-day wait didn't apply.

**Evidence:** The bucket boundaries and the seven-day interval from `db/postgres/004-stage-6-watch-chase.sql`. The key composition and the one-field fix in `stage_6_vendor_issue_key`, commit `0fcd62d`. The quieter consequence: the two-strike stop-chasing rule also counted per problem, so bucket changes reset it and a vendor could ignore six requests without tripping it.

**Takeaway:** Anything that chases vendors has a definition of "I already asked about this." Worth knowing what yours is. Mine was wrong for weeks and looked fine from outside.

**Framing discipline:** The point is how I find and fix things, not that my software had a bug. That's what someone hiring a builder is buying.

## 9. The locked PDF that disappears

**Format:** native video, 75 seconds · **Framework:** Hook / Body / Close, 0–3s hook · **Pillar:** build in public · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Show the password prompt in the first two seconds. State that you don't have the password.

**The turn:** This is where it quietly goes wrong in most offices. Someone can't open it, means to come back, doesn't. The vendor believes they sent it. The office believes it's handled.

**Evidence:** Upload `fixtures/pdfs/f18-password-protected.pdf` through the form. It doesn't crash, doesn't skip, doesn't guess — the row appears against Rapid Response Restoration LLC marked needs review, with the file attached. Routing behavior: `Route Password Protected PDF` in `n8n-workflows/stage-5-intake-workflow.json`.

**Takeaway:** The question isn't whether your process can open a locked file. It's whether you'd ever find out that it couldn't.

**Capture note:** Record in the same session as the demo — the board is staged either way.

## 10. The checkbox that proves someone ticked a checkbox

**Format:** text, 200 words · **Structure:** false belief → the fact → what it changes · **Pillar:** industry literacy · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Open on the thing everyone treats as confirmation: the additional insured box, ticked.

**The turn:** A tick tells you a tick was made. Actual additional-insured status depends on the endorsement language in the policy, which the certificate does not contain.

**Evidence:** Texas DOI guidance that additional-insured and cancellation-notice wording depend on policy or endorsement terms. The naming decision in my own build: the field is `addl_insd_asserted`, not `additional_insured`, because the honest name is the one that says who is claiming what.

**Takeaway:** If your process treats the box as proof, it's carrying weight the form was never built to hold.

## 11. The gap between two valid certificates

**Format:** single image — one timeline · **Structure:** image carries the claim, 100–150 words explain what it means · **Pillar:** industry literacy · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** The image does the work. Old policy ends. New policy starts two weeks later. Both certificates look perfectly fine.

**The turn:** The gap exists only in the space between two documents nobody puts side by side. Checking certificates one at a time cannot surface it, no matter how carefully you check.

**Evidence:** The `COVERAGE_GAP` check compares a renewal against what it replaces rather than reading it alone. Source: `scripts/lib/coi-stage-4-validator.js`.

**Takeaway:** It's a comparison problem, not a document problem. Different problems need different checks.

**Image discipline:** A plain timeline. Real artifact or plain diagram only — a decorative concept graphic here would read as marketing collateral and undo the positioning.

## 12. Seventeen vendors nobody was chasing

**Format:** native video, 60 seconds · **Framework:** Hook / Body / Close, 0–3s hook · **Pillar:** build in public · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** Two boards side by side. The old one looks completely normal.

**The turn:** It had been sending reminders for weeks and the main screen showed no trace of who'd been contacted. Adding one column revealed that seventeen of eighteen vendors had been silently flagged stop-chasing during earlier testing — behaving correctly, doing almost nothing, saying so nowhere.

**Evidence:** Logged in `progress-notes.md` under Open Behaviour Findings. The chase column now shows last contacted, follow-up due, request count, and paused state.

**Takeaway:** The dangerous failure isn't the one that throws an error. It's the one where something stops and the screen still looks fine. Whatever's missing from a dashboard is what nobody is watching.

## 13. What an automation is allowed to do without asking

**Format:** carousel, 8 slides · **Framework:** Problem-Proof · **Pillar:** operating model · **CTA:** soft — invite the question about their own systems

**Why Problem-Proof over Rant Callout:** the rant framework superficially fits an opinion post, but it is polarizing by design, and conviction-as-content is the wrong register for someone with no clients yet. Problem-Proof opens on a claim with a number — zero messages have left without a person clicking approve — and closes on the approval email as the receipt.

**Hook angle:** Open on the question to ask any automation vendor, which is not "how accurate is it."

**The turn:** The real question is what it can do without a person seeing it first. Accuracy is a spectrum; permission is a design decision, and it's the one that decides whether a bad day is an inconvenience or an incident.

**Evidence:** How I draw the line across builds. It can read, extract, sort, flag, draft, and notify on its own. It cannot send to a third party, change a status that implies a judgment, or decide something a person would be accountable for. Concretely: every outbound message waits for approval, the window expires rather than sending, outbound defaults to redirected during setup so a real vendor can't be emailed by accident, and two statuses can only ever be set by a human.

**Takeaway:** Ask where the line is drawn. If nobody can tell you, it hasn't been drawn.

**Why this is an operating-model post:** It's the principle I apply to every build, not a feature of one. This is what someone is buying when they hire a person rather than a product.

## 14. The check that fails the build if it says "compliant"

**Format:** single image — screenshot · **Structure:** image carries the claim, 100–150 words explain what it means · **Pillar:** honest limitation · **CTA:** none

**Hook angle:** The screenshot is the post. A failing check, and the four words that cause it.

**The turn:** Four words are banned from anything this system renders: compliant, verified, covered, approved. If any appears, it doesn't ship. Software that tells you a vendor is compliant is claiming something it cannot possibly know.

**Evidence:** The check in `scripts/verify-stage-7-dashboard.ps1`, plus the runtime guard in the dashboard node that throws rather than render a blocked term. Status is always a date fact instead: current until a date, expires in n days, expired n days ago, no certificate on file, needs review.

**Takeaway:** It was easier to make the claim impossible to ship than to keep remembering not to make it.

**Commentary length:** 100–150 words. The image carries it.

## 15. What I need from you before I build anything

**Format:** text, 250 words · **Structure:** `offers` skill — what they get, what it costs them, what makes it safe to say yes · **Pillar:** operating model · **CTA:** real — the call

**Hook angle:** Open on what I don't need: access to your property management system, a project plan, or a budget conversation.

**The turn:** What I do need is small and unglamorous — the list of vendors, the legal names that should appear on paperwork, the mailbox where documents already arrive, and one person who can say yes to an email. That's the whole input.

**Evidence:** Drawn from `docs/stage-9-client-onboarding-checklist.md`: vendor list, holder entities, requirements by trade, the mailbox, an approver. Plus the honest boundary from `docs/stage-9-data-processing-retention.md` — what I receive, where it lives, how long it stays, when it's deleted.

**Takeaway:** The first useful output is a picture of where things actually stand, and getting there takes days, not a quarter.

**The CTA:** This is the one post that asks directly. The offer is the diagnostic, not the build — send ten vendor certificates and get back a one-page picture of what's current, what's expired, what's missing, and what needs a look. No system access, no cost, and if the answer is "you're fine," that's what it says.

**Data handling:** Address it in the post, briefly. Asking someone to email real vendor documents to a stranger overseas is the moment the objection becomes real, and naming the retention terms unprompted turns the weakest moment into another proof point.

# Sequencing and cadence

**Publishing order is the numbering above.** Posts 1–5 while the audience is still mostly the wrong one. Posts 6–15 once comments and profile visits start coming from the industry.

**Before driving anyone to the profile, have three posts up:** 1, 2, and 3. Those answer the three questions a stranger asks in order — what world is this, is it real, is this person going to sell me garbage.

**Cadence:** one a week to start, rising to two or three as the audience shifts. Fifteen posts covers roughly four months at that pace, which is the right runway for the fourth build to produce its own material.

**Rotation across builds:** never two consecutive build-in-public posts about the same system. With four builds, each surfaces roughly monthly.

# Voice and checks

**Register:** incident report. Investigate, report precisely, state impact without drama, don't oversell. Short declaratives, real numbers, understated conclusions, first person singular, US spelling.

**Banned:** hype vocabulary (game-changer, unlock, leverage, seamless, powerful, revolutionize, robust). LinkedIn broetry — one line per paragraph. "Let that sink in." Rhetorical questions as openers, though they're fine mid-post. Emoji. More than 3–5 hashtags. Any selling in the body of a post that isn't 15. The four product-forbidden words in any claiming sense.

**Checks before publishing any draft:**

| Check | Standard |
|---|---|
| Forbidden words | Zero: compliant, verified, covered, approved |
| Hype vocabulary | Zero |
| First 210 characters | Must stand alone and earn the expand. Measure by truncating, not by eye |
| Length | Text 150–300 words · carousel slides under 25 words · video voiceover 150–200 words per 75 seconds |
| Factual trace | Every number, flag, and citation resolves to a named file or source |
| Product language | Nothing implying a tool being sold. It's work done inside a client's business |
| Em dashes | At most one per post. The single most reliable AI tell, and the hardest to notice while writing. Use commas, colons, or parentheses instead |
| AI writing tells | Run the full list in `seo-audit/references/ai-writing-detection.md`: banned openers ("in today's", "let's delve"), transitions ("that being said", "at its core"), closers ("in conclusion", "at the end of the day"), and structural patterns ("it's not just X, it's Y", "whether you're a X, Y, or Z", sentences opening with "By" plus a gerund) |
| Sentence length variance | Not every sentence the same length. Uniform rhythm reads as machine-written even when the words are clean |
| Read aloud | Anything that can't be said out loud without embarrassment gets cut. Ask: would I say this to a colleague? |
