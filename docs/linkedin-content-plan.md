# LinkedIn Content Plan — Vendor COI Build

Created: 2026-08-01

Fifteen posts drawn from this build. Descriptions only — no copy written yet.

**Cadence:** 2–3 per month across roughly six months. Never two consecutive weeks in the build-in-public slot, or this build starts crowding out the other three.

**Split:** 6 text · 4 native video · 3 carousel · 2 single image

**Pillar balance:** 4 build in public · 5 industry literacy · 4 honest limitation · 1 teardown · 1 question

Build in public is capped at 4 deliberately. That pillar is 20% of total output shared across all four builds — roughly 16 slots over six months, so this build gets a quarter of them. The teardown count is low for the same structural reason: teardowns are about problems you haven't solved, so they have to come from the problem inventory rather than from here.

The full demo video and its short cuts are listed separately at the end. They are sales assets, not part of these fifteen.

## Text posts (6)

### 1. The reminder that fired twice in two days
*Build in public · no CTA*

A certificate expiring in fifteen days triggered a chase email on day zero and another on day one, because crossing the fourteen-day line made the system treat it as a brand new problem rather than the same one moving closer.

**Reader takeaway:** whatever chases your vendors — software or a person with a calendar — has some definition of "this is a repeat." Most people have never checked what that definition is, and the failure mode is annoying the vendor you need something from.

### 2. The system that knows when to stop
*Honest limitation · no CTA*

After two ignored requests it stops emailing the vendor and tells a person instead. It does not escalate, it does not increase frequency, it does not keep going politely forever.

**Reader takeaway:** most chasing tools are built to persist. The useful question is what yours does on the fourth unanswered email, because that's where an automated reminder turns into a relationship problem.

### 3. Nobody has to tell you when a policy is cancelled
*Industry literacy · soft CTA*

The 2010 ACORD revision removed the carrier's obligation to notify certificate holders of cancellation. A certificate can show twelve months of coverage on a policy that ended in March, and no one is required to say a word.

**Reader takeaway:** the certificate in your file proves what was true on the day it was issued and nothing after. Most property managers have never been told this.

### 4. The checkbox that proves someone ticked a checkbox
*Industry literacy · no CTA*

Additional insured and waiver of subrogation appear as ticked boxes on the certificate, but actual status depends on the endorsement language in the policy. Per Texas DOI guidance, the box is an assertion on a form, not a fact about coverage.

**Reader takeaway:** if your process treats a ticked box as confirmation, you are relying on something that was never designed to carry that weight.

### 5. Apex Plumbing Services and Apex Plumbing and Heating
*Industry literacy · no CTA*

Two vendor names one keystroke apart, possibly two entirely separate companies. Automatic matching either merges them — quietly attributing one company's insurance to another — or rejects the match and files a duplicate. The related trap: certificates naming the owner LLC instead of the management company, both legitimate.

**Reader takeaway:** vendor name matching looks trivial and is the place where a register silently becomes wrong.

### 6. What would you hand over tomorrow?
*Question · no CTA*

Ask property managers which recurring admin task they'd give away first if it just did itself. Then publish what comes back as a follow-up post.

**Reader takeaway:** none — this one is for you. It is market research for build #3 and the follow-up post is usually stronger than the question.

## Native video (4)

Each 60–120 seconds, screen recording with voiceover, captions burned in. No face required.

The test for all four: if the takeaway is about my software, it's a bad video. If it's about the viewer's operation, it's a good one.

### 7. The locked PDF that disappears
*Build in public · no CTA*

Drag a password-protected certificate in. It doesn't crash, it doesn't guess, it puts the row in front of a human.

**Reader takeaway:** some of your vendors send locked PDFs. If your process has no defined path for them, they vanish silently, and nobody notices for a year.

### 8. Two buttons before anything leaves
*Build in public · no CTA*

The approval email arrives, shows the vendor and the exact message, and waits. Click approve, watch it send and get logged.

**Reader takeaway:** the question to ask any automation vendor is not "is it accurate," it's "what can it send without a person seeing it first."

### 9. Seventeen vendors nobody was chasing
*Build in public · no CTA*

The old board beside the new one. The system had been drafting and sending reminders for weeks and the main screen showed no trace of who had been contacted. Adding one column revealed that seventeen of eighteen vendors had been silently flagged "stop chasing" during earlier testing — behaving correctly, doing almost nothing, and saying so nowhere.

**Reader takeaway:** the dangerous failure isn't the one that throws an error, it's the one where something quietly stops and the screen still looks normal. Whatever's missing from your dashboard is the thing nobody has been watching.

### 10. Where the six hours actually go
*Teardown · no CTA*

Narrated over the register: the specific minutes a mid-size manager loses to vendor certificate paperwork in a month, and which of them are the second and third follow-up nobody counts.

**Reader takeaway:** the cost isn't the filing, it's the chasing — and it's invisible because each individual instance is four minutes.

## Document carousels (3)

Six to ten slides each, designed properly, two or three lines of post copy above to earn the swipe.

### 11. There is exactly one place this can be wrong
*Honest limitation · no CTA*

Slides walk the path a certificate takes from arrival to filed, and mark the single step where a wrong answer could enter: reading the document. Every decision after that is fixed logic that behaves identically every time.

**Reader takeaway:** ask any vendor to point at the one place their system can be wrong. If they can't, they haven't thought about it. (Lead with the risk question, not the technology — your buyers don't care what's under it.)

### 12. Everything that can be wrong with a certificate
*Honest limitation · no CTA*

The full checklist. Nine problems that stop a certificate cold and put it in front of a person — inverted dates, a locked file, a document that isn't a certificate at all, a vendor name too close to an existing one. Five that get filed with a visible note instead: holder mismatch, a limit under your minimum, a missing general liability line, a missing workers' comp line, a coverage gap. One slide each, plus the reasoning for the split.

Leave out the two internal failure modes (the reader doesn't care that the model refused or returned something malformed) — that's twelve slides of things they can actually check by hand.

**Reader takeaway:** a system with only pass and fail trains you to ignore it, because everything minor becomes an alarm. The design question is which of these deserves to interrupt you. Save-and-reference content.

### 13. What a certificate is, and what it isn't
*Industry literacy · no CTA*

A certificate of insurance is informational and does not amend the policy — that language is on the form itself. Slides cover what it does evidence, what it doesn't, and which questions belong with the broker instead.

**Reader takeaway:** most offices treat the certificate as the coverage. It's a receipt describing coverage, issued on one day, by someone who isn't the insurer.

## Single image (2)

The image is the post. Strip it and nothing remains.

Real artifacts only — a screenshot of actual work, or one plain diagram carrying a single idea. No decorative or concept graphics: that format is what offshore automation spam looks like, and it undoes the positioning faster than it earns reach.

### 14. The gap between two valid certificates
*Industry literacy · no CTA*

One timeline. Old policy ends, new policy starts two weeks later, both certificates look perfectly fine on their own. The gap exists only in the space between two documents nobody puts side by side.

**Reader takeaway:** checking certificates one at a time cannot surface this. It's a comparison problem, not a document problem. (One idea, one image — a carousel here would be padding.)

### 15. The build that fails if it says "compliant"
*Honest limitation · no CTA*

A screenshot of the check that refuses to ship if the rendered page contains *compliant*, *verified*, *covered*, or *approved*, plus the failure output. Roughly 150 words on why those four words are banned from software that tracks insurance paperwork.

**Reader takeaway:** software that tells you a vendor is compliant is claiming something it cannot possibly know. The fix was making it impossible to ship.
